# Part B — Academic Writing Rules

## Purpose

These rules govern the writing style, academic tone, scientific reasoning, citation practice and methodological language used when preparing Part B of the UEA MRes Clinical Science dissertation.

The objective is high-quality, original, evidence-based academic writing that accurately reflects the researcher's actual work.

# 1. Academic Standard

Write at the standard expected of an MRes Clinical Science dissertation and, where appropriate, a manuscript prepared for scientific publication.

The writing should be:
- clear;
- precise;
- analytical;
- evidence based;
- appropriately cautious;
- concise;
- scientifically rigorous;
- readable;
- logically structured.

Avoid unnecessary complexity.

# 2. UK English

Use UK English consistently.

Examples:
- analyse, not analyze;
- organisation, not organization;
- centre, not center;
- programme, not program;
- modelling, not modeling;
- behaviour, not behavior;
- optimise, not optimize.

Follow the spelling conventions already used in the final Part A where appropriate.

# 3. Write for Meaning, Not Length

Do not increase word count by adding:
- generic background;
- repeated explanations;
- unnecessary transitions;
- obvious statements;
- textbook definitions;
- lengthy summaries of well-established concepts.

Each paragraph should have a clear scientific purpose.

# 4. Avoid Generic Filler

Avoid unnecessary phrases such as:
- "It is important to note that..."
- "It is worth noting that..."
- "In today's rapidly evolving..."
- "This highlights the importance of..."
- "Furthermore, it is important to consider..."
- "Taken together, these findings..."
- "In conclusion, it can be seen that..."
- "This underscores the critical importance..."
- "A growing body of literature..."
- "Against this backdrop..."
- "In the context of..."
- "It is increasingly recognised that..."
- "This is a complex and multifaceted issue..."
- "There are several important considerations..."
- "Overall, it can be said that..."

Use these only where genuinely necessary.

Prefer direct statements.

Example:

Avoid:

"Heart failure is a complex and multifaceted clinical condition that has a significant impact on patients."

Prefer:

"Heart failure is characterised by impaired cardiac function and is associated with substantial morbidity and mortality."

# 5. Do Not Force Transitions

Do not add a transition word to every sentence or paragraph.

Avoid repetitive use of:
- Furthermore
- Moreover
- Additionally
- However
- Therefore
- Consequently
- Nevertheless
- Thus
- In addition

Use transitions when they clarify logical relationships.

Scientific writing can move naturally between sentences without explicit linking words.

# 6. Sentence Structure

Prefer clear sentences.

Avoid unnecessarily long sentences containing several independent ideas.

Where a sentence contains multiple claims, consider separating them.

Avoid excessive use of:
- semicolons;
- parenthetical statements;
- em dashes;
- nested clauses.

However, do not make every sentence artificially short.

Use natural variation.

# 7. Paragraph Structure

Each paragraph should generally:
1. introduce one main idea;
2. provide relevant evidence;
3. explain the significance;
4. connect to the next scientific point where necessary.

Avoid paragraphs containing multiple unrelated topics.

# 8. Evidence Before Interpretation

Distinguish between:

### Established evidence
What published research demonstrates.

### Interpretation
What those findings may mean.

### Hypothesis
What the present study predicts.

### Finding
What the present study actually demonstrates.

Do not mix these categories.

# 9. Do Not Predict Results

Before SPSS analysis is complete, never write statements implying the direction of the study's findings.

Do not write:

"Patients with AS had higher PVA."

Do not write:

"Myocardial efficiency was reduced in AS."

Do not write:

"The analysis demonstrated a significant difference."

Instead use:

"The study was designed to assess whether PVA differed between the groups."

or:

`[INSERT FINAL RESULT AFTER SPSS ANALYSIS]`

# 10. Distinguish Planned and Completed Work

The research proposal describes the planned methodology.

The dissertation must describe what was actually completed.

Never convert proposed methodology into completed methodology without evidence.

Use:

`[VERIFY]`

if uncertain.

# 11. Sample Size Language

The completed dataset contains:
- 5 AS cases;
- 5 controls.

Use terms such as:
- exploratory analysis;
- preliminary analysis;
- hypothesis-generating analysis;

where appropriate.

Avoid describing the study as:
- definitive;
- adequately powered;

unless this is explicitly demonstrated.

Do not overstate generalisability.

# 12. Research Question

The research question must be:
- specific;
- answerable;
- aligned with the actual dataset;
- consistent with the Research Proposal;
- consistent with Part A.

Do not introduce unrelated research questions.

# 13. Aim

The overall aim should be one clear statement.

It should describe what the study seeks to investigate.

Do not include multiple unrelated aims.

Do not claim outcomes that were not measured.

# 14. Objectives

Objectives should be:
- specific;
- measurable;
- aligned with the methodology;
- achievable using the actual dataset.

Do not include an objective if it was not performed.

# 15. Hypothesis

The hypothesis must be predictive rather than retrospective.

Use language such as:

"was hypothesised to..."

or:

"the study hypothesised that..."

Do not write the hypothesis as though the result is already known.

# 16. Methods Writing

Methods should be factual rather than argumentative.

Describe:
- what was done;
- how it was done;
- what data were used;
- how measurements were obtained;
- how outcomes were defined.

Avoid excessive justification within Methods unless required to understand the methodology.

# 17. MASS and CMR Contouring

Use terminology consistently.

Where manual contouring was performed, state this clearly.

Do not describe manual segmentation as fully automated.

Where contours were manually corrected, say so if supported by the project documentation.

Do not invent software versions or technical parameters.

# 18. PV-Loop Terminology

Maintain the distinction between:

### Stroke Work (SW)
Mechanical work performed during ventricular ejection.

### Pressure–Volume Area (PVA)
A measure representing total mechanical energy associated with the pressure–volume relationship.

### Myocardial Efficiency
The relationship between external mechanical work and total mechanical energy.

Do not use these terms interchangeably.

# 19. 4D-Flow Terminology

Maintain the distinction between:

### Myocardial mechanical energetics
Derived from ventricular pressure–volume relationships.

### Blood-flow energetics
Derived from 4D-flow CMR, such as kinetic energy and viscous energy loss.

Do not describe blood-flow kinetic energy as equivalent to myocardial mechanical work.

# 20. Statistical Language

Until SPSS analysis is complete:

Do not use:
- significant;
- non-significant;
- associated;
- correlated;
- increased;
- decreased;
- higher;
- lower;

to describe the present study's findings.

These may be used when discussing published literature, provided the cited evidence supports them.

# 21. Results Writing

Results should be descriptive and objective.

Do not interpret findings extensively in the Results section.

Interpretation belongs primarily in Discussion.

Do not repeat every number in prose if the table already communicates it clearly.

# 22. Discussion Writing

The Discussion should not simply repeat the Results.

It should explain:
- what the findings mean;
- whether they agree with previous research;
- why they may differ;
- physiological implications;
- clinical relevance;
- methodological considerations;
- limitations;
- future research.

Do not overinterpret small exploratory findings.

# 23. Contradictory Evidence

Do not selectively cite only evidence supporting the study hypothesis.

Where relevant:
- acknowledge conflicting evidence;
- explain methodological differences;
- consider biological explanations;
- avoid forcing agreement.

Scientific disagreement is acceptable and should be presented objectively.

# 24. Clinical Claims

The completed study has only five participants per group.

Therefore, avoid claims such as:
- "This proves..."
- "This establishes..."
- "This demonstrates clinical utility..."
- "This should change clinical practice..."
- "This provides a new diagnostic tool..."

unless genuinely supported by the evidence.

Prefer:
- "These findings may support further investigation..."
- "The findings provide preliminary evidence..."
- "The results require validation in larger cohorts..."

Use cautious language proportional to the evidence.

# 25. Limitations

Limitations should be specific to the actual study.

Potential issues include:
- small sample size;
- reduced analysed cohort;
- retrospective design;
- single-centre data;
- potential selection bias;
- image quality;
- manual contouring;
- operator dependence;
- assumptions in non-invasive PV-loop reconstruction;
- incomplete availability of 4D-flow measures;
- limited generalisability.

Only include limitations that genuinely apply.

# 26. Strengths

Strengths should also be specific.

Possible strengths include:
- use of CMR;
- non-invasive energetic assessment;
- detailed LV contouring;
- integration of myocardial and flow energetics;
- direct comparison with healthy controls.

Do not claim strengths that cannot be demonstrated.

# 27. Citations

Follow the citation system used in final Part A.

Citations should:
- support the exact claim;
- be placed close to the claim;
- use appropriate primary or authoritative evidence;
- not be added merely for appearance.

Do not cite one paper for a paragraph containing several unrelated claims unless that paper genuinely supports them.

# 28. Reference Quality

Prefer:
- guidelines;
- consensus documents;
- systematic reviews;
- meta-analyses;
- peer-reviewed original studies;
- methodological papers.

Avoid low-quality sources unless there is a specific reason to use them.

# 29. Originality

All prose must be independently written.

Do not:
- copy sentences;
- reproduce distinctive phrases;
- closely mirror source sentence structure;
- perform superficial synonym replacement;
- reproduce another dissertation's wording.

Synthesize the evidence in original language.

# 30. Plagiarism Prevention

Before finalising each section, ask:

> Could this sentence reasonably be recognised as copied from a source?

If yes:
- rewrite it independently;
- ensure the citation remains appropriate;
- verify that the scientific meaning is unchanged.

Do not use direct quotations unless absolutely necessary.

# 31. Avoid Artificial "Humanisation"

Do not intentionally add:
- mistakes;
- awkward sentences;
- informal expressions;
- random sentence fragments;
- inconsistent terminology;
- unnecessary personal comments.

Natural academic writing comes from:
- specific content;
- accurate evidence;
- varied sentence structure;
- logical reasoning;
- precise language;
- genuine interpretation.

# 32. Avoid Excessive Hedging

Do not use phrases such as:
- "it might perhaps be possible that..."
- "it could potentially possibly..."
- "it may perhaps suggest..."

Use appropriate scientific uncertainty:
- "may";
- "could";
- "suggests";
- "is consistent with";

when justified.

# 33. Avoid Overstatement

Match language to evidence.

Use:

"was associated with"

rather than:

"caused"

unless causality is established.

Use:

"may indicate"

rather than:

"proves"

when evidence is preliminary.

# 34. Terminology Consistency

Use the same terminology throughout Part B and maintain consistency with Part A.

Examples:
- aortic stenosis (AS);
- cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR);
- pressure–volume (PV) loop;
- pressure–volume area (PVA);
- stroke work (SW);
- myocardial efficiency;
- four-dimensional flow CMR (4D-flow CMR);
- kinetic energy (KE);
- viscous energy loss (VEL), where this abbreviation is used and defined.

Define abbreviations at first use in Part B where required.

# 35. Tables

Tables should:
- have clear titles;
- use consistent units;
- avoid unnecessary decimal places;
- be understandable without excessive reference to the main text;
- follow UEA formatting guidance;
- use appropriate footnotes.

Do not create numerical tables until the actual data are available.

# 36. Figures

Figures should add information rather than duplicate the text.

Potential figures include:
- participant-selection flow diagram;
- CMR analysis workflow;
- representative contouring;
- PV-loop reconstruction;
- 4D-flow workflow;
- results plots.

Do not fabricate data or create illustrative findings that could be mistaken for actual results.

# 37. Results Placeholders

Before SPSS completion, use explicit placeholders:

`[INSERT RESULT]`

`[INSERT TABLE]`

`[INSERT FIGURE]`

`[INSERT P-VALUE]`

`[INSERT EFFECT SIZE]`

`[INSERT CONFIDENCE INTERVAL]`

Do not replace these with guessed values.

# 38. Discussion Placeholders

Use:

`[INSERT FINAL FINDING]`

`[COMPARE WITH RELEVANT LITERATURE AFTER RESULTS ARE AVAILABLE]`

where appropriate.

Do not predict whether the findings will support or contradict the hypothesis.

# 39. Abstract

The final abstract should not be completed until the Results are available.

Before then, create:
- Background;
- Aim;
- Methods;
- Results placeholder;
- Conclusion placeholder.

# 40. Final Conclusion

The final conclusion should only be completed after the Results and Discussion are finalised.

It must reflect the actual findings.

Do not write a definitive conclusion before the statistical analysis is complete.

# 41. Academic Integrity

Do not fabricate or conceal information.

Do not:
- invent data;
- invent methods;
- invent citations;
- invent ethics approvals;
- invent participant selection;
- invent supervisor decisions;
- invent results.

Where uncertain, state the uncertainty explicitly.

# 42. Final Writing Audit

Before considering any section complete, check:

### Scientific accuracy
- Are claims scientifically correct?
- Are they supported by evidence?

### Methodological accuracy
- Does the text describe what was actually done?

### Evidence
- Are citations appropriate?
- Are stronger sources available?

### Originality
- Is the wording independently written?
- Is there any close paraphrasing?

### Consistency
- Does the section agree with Part A?
- Does it agree with the Research Proposal?
- Does it agree with the current project status?

### Scope
- Are claims proportional to n=5 + n=5?

### Style
- UK English?
- Clear?
- Concise?
- Natural?
- No unnecessary filler?

### Integrity
- No fabricated results?
- No fabricated references?
- No unsupported methodology?

# 43. Priority Order

When making writing decisions, prioritise:

1. Accuracy
2. Scientific evidence
3. Fidelity to actual research
4. UEA requirements
5. Logical structure
6. Clear language
7. Concision
8. Presentation

Never sacrifice accuracy to improve apparent fluency.
