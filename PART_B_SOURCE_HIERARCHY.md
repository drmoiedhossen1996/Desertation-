# Part B — Source Hierarchy and Evidence Policy

## Purpose

This document establishes which sources should be prioritised when preparing Part B of the UEA MRes Clinical Science dissertation.

The purpose is to prevent outdated drafts, conflicting versions, unsupported assumptions or general knowledge from overriding the final project documentation.

# Overall Source Hierarchy

When information conflicts, use the following hierarchy:

## Level 1 — UEA Official Guidance
Use Repository 2 for:
- dissertation structure;
- formatting;
- assessment;
- presentation;
- word limits;
- institutional requirements.

## Level 2 — Final Project Documents
Use Repository 3 for:
- research question;
- approved aims;
- objectives;
- hypothesis;
- study design;
- proposed methodology;
- project-specific terminology;
- project-specific outcomes.

## Level 3 — Final Part A Literature Review
Use the final Part A in Repository 3 for:
- established literature synthesis;
- knowledge gap;
- evidence base;
- terminology;
- relevant references;
- conceptual framework.

Part A is the principal literature foundation for Part B.

## Level 4 — Research Proposal
Use the Research Proposal for:
- planned study methodology;
- planned participant cohort;
- inclusion/exclusion criteria;
- planned outcome measures;
- planned analytical framework;
- original rationale.

However:

**The Research Proposal describes the planned study, not necessarily everything that was ultimately completed.**

The actual dissertation must distinguish planned work from completed work.

## Level 5 — Earlier Part A Drafts
Use Repository 1 only when useful for:
- tracing earlier material;
- locating references;
- identifying development of figures/tables;
- understanding earlier drafts.

Do not allow an old draft to override the final Part A.

## Level 6 — General Academic Knowledge
General scientific knowledge may be used only where:
- the project documents do not provide sufficient information;
- the statement is established scientific knowledge;
- it does not contradict the project documents.

Where a scientific claim requires evidence, prefer a peer-reviewed citation rather than relying solely on general knowledge.

# Conflict Resolution

When two sources conflict:
1. Identify the conflict.
2. Determine which source has higher authority.
3. Prefer the higher-ranked source.
4. Do not silently alter information.
5. If the conflict cannot be resolved, flag it.

Use:

`[SOURCE CONFLICT — VERIFY]`

when necessary.

# Research Proposal vs Actual Study

This distinction is critical.

The Research Proposal describes what was intended.

The final dissertation must describe what was actually done.

For every major methodological statement, ask:

> Is this documented as having actually been performed?

If yes:
- include it.

If uncertain:
- use `[VERIFY]`.

If no:
- do not describe it as completed.

# Planned Cohort vs Completed Cohort

The Research Proposal originally planned:
- 28 AS cases;
- 10 healthy controls.

The completed dissertation analysis currently contains:
- 5 AS cases;
- 5 healthy controls.

The dissertation must clearly distinguish these.

Never use the Research Proposal's planned sample size as though it represents the final analysed sample.

# Evidence Hierarchy for Scientific Claims

When making scientific claims in Part B, prioritise:
1. Systematic reviews/meta-analyses where appropriate
2. Major clinical guidelines
3. Consensus statements
4. High-quality prospective studies
5. High-quality retrospective studies
6. Relevant CMR studies
7. Relevant 4D-flow CMR studies
8. Relevant PV-loop/myocardial energetics studies
9. Methodological papers
10. Other peer-reviewed evidence

Use the strongest appropriate evidence for each claim.

# Evidence Matching

Every important scientific claim should be supported by evidence appropriate to that claim.

Examples:

Clinical recommendations:
→ guideline/consensus statement

Disease prevalence:
→ epidemiological study or authoritative report

Pathophysiological mechanism:
→ relevant mechanistic/original research or high-quality review

CMR methodology:
→ methodological/validation paper

4D-flow methodology:
→ appropriate 4D-flow methodological/consensus literature

PV-loop methodology:
→ appropriate methodological/original research

Study findings from another paper:
→ cite the original paper whenever possible.

# Citation Policy

Use the citation style established in the final Part A.

Before drafting Part B:
1. Inspect the final Part A.
2. Identify its citation format.
3. Use the same system.
4. Maintain the same reference-list style.

Citations must be placed close to the claim they support.

Do not add citations merely to make paragraphs appear academic.

Do not cite a source unless it actually supports the statement.

# Reference Integrity

Never fabricate:
- authors;
- article titles;
- journal names;
- volume;
- issue;
- pages;
- DOI;
- PMID;
- publication year.

If a reference cannot be verified:

`[REFERENCE REQUIRES VERIFICATION]`

If additional evidence is genuinely required:

`[NEW HIGH-QUALITY REFERENCE REQUIRED]`

# Source Wording and Originality

Do not copy sentences or distinctive phrases from:
- journal articles;
- guidelines;
- websites;
- grant applications;
- exemplars;
- dissertations;
- Part A drafts.

Do not perform superficial word substitution.

Read and understand the source, then write the scientific idea independently.

The purpose is evidence synthesis, not source reproduction.

# Part A and Part B Relationship

Part A is the comprehensive literature review.

Part B should use Part A as its evidence foundation.

Do not reproduce long sections of Part A.

Do not unnecessarily repeat the same literature discussion.

Part B should move from:

literature and knowledge gap

to:

specific research question

to:

actual study

to:

actual findings.

# External Literature

External literature may be used if needed to strengthen Part B.

When using external literature:
- prioritise peer-reviewed sources;
- prefer primary research for specific findings;
- use recognised guidelines for recommendations;
- avoid low-quality websites;
- verify bibliographic details;
- ensure the source genuinely supports the claim.

Clearly distinguish newly introduced evidence from evidence already established in Part A.

# No Unsupported Claims

If the available sources do not support a statement:

Do not invent support.

Instead use:

`[EVIDENCE REQUIRED]`

or remove the claim.

# Final Evidence Audit

Before finalising Part B, check:
- Is every major scientific claim evidence based?
- Does each citation support the exact claim?
- Are primary sources used where appropriate?
- Are guidelines cited for guideline statements?
- Are methodological claims supported?
- Are references genuine?
- Are citations consistent with Part A?
- Are there any unsupported claims?
- Are there unnecessary citations?
- Has source wording been independently paraphrased?
- Are conflicting sources identified?
